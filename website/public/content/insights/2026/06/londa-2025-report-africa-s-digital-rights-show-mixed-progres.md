# LONDA 2025 Report: Africa's Digital Rights Show Mixed Progress Amid Authoritarian Pushback

**TL;DR:** Paradigm Initiative's LONDA 2025 report reveals stark digital rights divide across 29 African countries, with some nations enacting progressive privacy laws while others intensify internet shutdowns, surveillance, and activist arrests.

## Key Points

- LONDA 2025 report evaluated digital rights across 29 African countries using 12-indicator scoring system out of 60 points
- Botswana emerged as success story, jumping from 28 to 35 points after implementing Data Protection Act and maintaining zero internet shutdowns
- Multiple countries deployed internet shutdowns during political tension: Cameroon (Oct-Nov elections), Tanzania (October elections with 1,700+ arrests)
- ECOWAS Court condemned Senegal's 2023 internet blackouts, establishing legal precedent that kept Senegal's internet open in 2025
- Zambian government cancelled RightsCon 2026 less than a week before event, demonstrating how quickly digital rights progress can be reversed
- Governments weaponize cybercrime laws against dissent: Benin arrested newspaper editors, Somalia targeted journalists, Ghana misapplied Electronic Communications Act
- Technology-facilitated gender-based violence rising, with gendered disinformation campaigns in Benin targeting women politicians
- African Commission passed Resolution 630 in 2025 guiding tech companies on information integrity and human rights assessments
- Surveillance expansion in Zimbabwe, Lesotho, and Togo lacks judicial oversight, forcing self-censorship among journalists and activists

## Deep Dive

## Continental Digital Rights Landscape

The LONDA 2025 report exposes Africa's digital rights as a tale of two continents. While organizations like the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights push progressive frameworks through resolutions like Resolution 580 (keeping internet open during elections) and Resolution 630 (tech company accountability), ground-level implementation remains inconsistent. The scoring methodology provides quantifiable evidence of this divide, with Botswana's 7-point jump contrasting sharply with Sudan's decline to an "abysmal" 20 points amid ongoing conflict.

## Systematic Oppression Patterns

Governments across the continent are deploying remarkably similar playbooks for digital oppression. The pattern includes: (1) internet shutdowns during politically sensitive periods (Cameroon's Oct-Nov election blackout, Tanzania's total digital blackout with mass arrests), (2) weaponized cybercrime legislation targeting journalists and activists under "false news" pretenses, and (3) expanding surveillance infrastructure without judicial oversight. The Zambian government's last-minute RightsCon cancellation demonstrates how quickly authoritarian reflexes can override years of digital rights progress.

## Technical and Legal Countermeasures

The ECOWAS Court's condemnation of Senegal's internet shutdowns represents a critical legal precedent that freedom tech builders should monitor for replication across other regional courts. However, the report reveals that legal frameworks consistently lag behind technological oppression - most nations lack adequate protections against technology-facilitated gender-based violence, and surveillance programs operate without meaningful judicial oversight. The expansion of cybersecurity centers (like Togo's new facility) often serves as cover for enhanced monitoring capabilities rather than genuine security improvements.

## Implications for Freedom Tech Development

This systematic documentation reveals where freedom tech solutions are most urgently needed: circumventing election-period shutdowns, protecting journalist communications from device seizure and inspection, and providing privacy tools for vulnerable groups facing targeted surveillance. The report's emphasis on Universal Service Fund (USF) implementation also highlights infrastructure gaps that mesh networking and offline-capable tools could address in rural areas.
